# Video 3 (EN): How airlines really make money and why flights can be so cheap
# One line = one scene = one image. General explanation, no exact figures.
A flight from Berlin to Mallorca for nineteen euros. Less than your dinner. How can that possibly be worth it?
A plane costs millions, fuel is expensive, and the crew wants to be paid. Still, airlines fly thousands of flights every single day.
The honest answer is surprising: with the ticket alone, airlines often earn almost nothing.
In this video we look at how airlines really make money, from the seat itself all the way to frequent flyer miles.
Phase one: the brutal math of a single flight.
Fuel is one of the biggest cost blocks. A large plane burns several tons of kerosene every hour.
On top of that come salaries for pilots and crew, maintenance, airport fees and insurance.
And the plane itself is either bought with expensive loans, or leased with monthly payments.
In the end, many airlines are left with a very small profit margin after all costs, often only a few percent.
That means even small swings in the oil price or in how full the planes are can turn profit into loss very quickly.
Phase two: why every single seat counts.
A flight costs almost the same whether ten or two hundred people are on board. An empty seat earns zero euros but still costs money.
That is why airlines watch the load factor. When a plane is eighty or ninety percent full, it starts to get really interesting.
The trick behind it is called yield management. It is dynamic pricing.
The same seat costs little when booked early and often much more shortly before departure.
This way airlines get more money from business travelers and fill the remaining seats with cheaper tickets.
Some airlines even overbook on purpose, because experience shows that a few passengers never show up.
Phase three: the money is in the extras.
Baggage, seat selection, snacks, priority boarding: every little thing has its own price.
The ticket lures you in, the extras make the profit. These are called ancillary revenues.
Low-cost airlines especially live on this. The base price is low, but you pay extra for almost everything.
And the onboard menu, duty-free sales and even advertising on the plane bring in extra money too.
Phase four: the low-cost model.
Low-cost airlines save at every corner. They usually fly only one aircraft type, which keeps maintenance and training simple.
They often land at smaller, cheaper airports that are farther away from the city.
And they turn the plane around on the ground extremely fast, because a plane sitting on the ground earns no money.
On top of that come tighter seat rows, no free food and as little staff as possible on each flight.
Phase five: networks, hubs and miles.
Big airlines build a hub. From there they fly in all directions, and connecting passengers fill the planes.
In addition, many passenger planes carry cargo in the belly of the aircraft. That adds extra revenue.
And then there are the frequent flyer programs. This is where it gets really interesting.
Banks and other companies buy miles from the airline and hand them to their customers. The airline gets money in advance.
At some airlines the loyalty programs are even worth more than the flying business itself. Analysts say so too.
Phase six: what this means for you.
When you know how the system works, you can book smart. Book early, stay flexible and skip the extras deliberately.
Always compare the total price, not just the ticket price. With low-cost airlines a flight with luggage can suddenly cost more than a regular one.
And if you collect miles, do it only when you fly a lot anyway. Otherwise the effort rarely pays off.
Most important: behind a cheap ticket there is no miracle, but a cleverly designed business model.
And now you know how small extra fees and full seats add up to a profit in the end.
