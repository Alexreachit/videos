# Video 3: Your life as every rank, from intern to CEO
# One line = one scene = one image. Numbers are rough examples; they differ a lot by country, company and industry. No financial advice.
It is Monday morning, you walk into a huge office building for the first time, and nobody knows your name yet.
By the end of this video you will have lived through seven ranks, from the very first desk to the corner office.
At every rank your hours, your stress, your pay and your power will change, and not always the way you expect.
All numbers here are rough examples. They depend heavily on the country, the company and the industry.
So take a deep breath, grab your badge, and let us climb the ladder.
Phase one: the intern.
You start at the very bottom. Your badge works on exactly two doors, and one of them is the kitchen.
Your tasks are small. You make copies, you book meeting rooms, and you take notes in meetings you barely understand.
Pay is low, sometimes only a few hundred euros a month, and in some places an internship is not paid at all.
But here is the secret nobody tells you: an intern who asks good questions is remembered long after the internship ends.
You learn how the company really works, who decides things, and which people actually get things done.
Most interns will never reach the top. The ones who do usually did one simple thing: they made themselves useful early.
After a few months, someone tells you they have an opening. You have just moved up a rank.
Phase two: the junior employee.
Now you have a real contract and your first real salary, often somewhere around three to four thousand euros a month before tax.
You also have real deadlines. For the first time, a mistake of yours can cost the company actual money.
Your days are full of spreadsheets, emails and small projects. Nobody thanks you, but everybody notices when you are late.
This is the rank where most people quietly decide who they want to become. The curious ones learn everything they can.
The comfortable ones stop learning after the first year. Both are paid almost the same, until one day they are not.
You work hard, you stay late a few times, and you learn to say yes without agreeing to everything.
After two or three solid years, your boss asks you to take on more. Another rank is waiting.
Phase three: the senior employee.
Now you are the person others come to with questions. Your expertise is your power, and your calendar starts to fill up.
Your salary rises, often into the range of five to seven thousand euros a month, and you finally feel a little financial calm.
You mentor the new people, you fix the problems nobody else can fix, and you start to see the company from above.
But there is a trap at this rank. Many people become very good at their job and then stay there for twenty years.
Being the best specialist feels safe. But the next rank does not reward being the best at tasks, it rewards leading people.
You have to choose: the expert path, or the leadership path. Both are respectable, but they lead to very different lives.
You choose leadership. Someone hands you your first team, and suddenly your job changes completely.
Phase four: the team lead.
You no longer do most of the work yourself. Your job is to make sure six other people can do their work well.
Your day turns into meetings, one-on-one talks, budgets and small conflicts. You sometimes miss the quiet, simple work.
Your pay goes up, but the real change is the responsibility. When your team fails, you are the one who answers for it.
This is where many people find out that managing people is a different skill from doing the job.
The good leaders listen first. They protect their team from pointless pressure and say thank you out loud.
The bad leaders micromanage, take the credit and wonder why their best people quietly leave.
You do it well, your team delivers, and one day the leadership asks you to run an entire department.
Phase five: the director.
Now you lead other leaders. You rarely see the actual work. You see numbers, plans and the people who explain them to you.
You decide budgets worth hundreds of thousands, sometimes millions. A single signature of yours can start or stop a project.
Politics becomes part of the job. You need allies, you need to know who is rising and who is falling, and you learn to read a room.
Your salary can reach well into six figures, often with a bonus that depends on how the whole department performs.
But the price is time. Evenings, weekends and holidays are no longer fully yours, because the phone never really stops.
Many directors admit that this is the rank where they gave up the most private life for the least visible thanks.
Still, you perform, you build trust at the top, and eventually the executive floor opens its doors for you.
Phase six: the vice president.
You now run a whole business area. Hundreds of people work, indirectly, for the decisions you make in a single meeting.
You travel, you present to the board, and you are measured by results that you can only influence, not control.
Your income is high, often a base salary plus a large bonus, and sometimes shares of the company on top.
But the pressure is huge. If the quarter goes badly, everyone looks at you, and the people above you are always watching.
Only a very small share of employees ever get here. And among the vice presidents, only a few get to the very top.
Those few usually have three things in common: results, relationships, and the willingness to carry the risk.
One day the board calls you in, and the chair of the board says a sentence you have waited for all your life.
Phase seven: the CEO.
You are the face of the company. Every word you say can move the share price, and every mistake becomes a headline.
You earn more than anyone else in the building, often hundreds of times a junior salary, but you hardly ever have a free evening.
You answer to the board, to the shareholders and to thousands of employees who all want something different from you.
And here is the surprising part: your power is smaller than it looks. You depend on your team, your board and the market.
Many CEOs say that the title is not the reward. The reward is being able to shape something much bigger than yourself.
But they also say it can be a very lonely seat, because almost everybody around you wants something from you.
So was the climb worth it? That depends on what you want from your life, not on the title on your door.
Some people are happiest as a senior expert with a calm evening. Others need the corner office. Both are fine.
What matters is that you choose the rank on purpose, instead of drifting up or down without ever deciding.
