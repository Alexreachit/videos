# Video 6: Why not sleeping can kill you (hour by hour)
# One line = one scene = one image (about 30 words per scene, 12+ minutes). Facts from public studies and reports; numbers are rough and differ from person to person. Not medical advice.
It is two in the morning, your eyes burn, and you tell yourself that one more hour will not hurt. This is the story of what that small choice really does to your body. Maybe you have been there before, and maybe you are watching this exact video way past your bedtime.
By the end of this video you will have lived through seven stages of sleeplessness, from your first missed night all the way to the point where the human body simply cannot go on. Grab a blanket, because you may get sleepy.
Sleep feels like wasted time, and our culture often treats it like a weakness. Yet scientists have never found a single animal that can live completely without it. Even dolphins sleep, one half of the brain at a time.
Before we start, a quick note: this video is entertainment and general information, not medical advice. If you struggle with sleep, please talk to a doctor. We want you to enjoy the story, and to look after yourself afterwards.
Everything here comes from public studies, medical reports and famous experiments. The numbers are rough examples, and they differ from person to person.
Your brain does not switch off at night. It sorts memories, repairs tissue, and resets the hormones that control your hunger, your mood and your stress. That is a lot of work for something that looks like doing nothing.
Researchers found that during deep sleep the fluid around your brain flushes out waste, including proteins linked to diseases like Alzheimer's. Skip the night, and the cleaning crew never arrives.
So how long could you really stay awake, and what exactly would be the thing that finally breaks? Let us find out, hour by hour. The answer is stranger than most people expect.
Remember one number as we go: eleven days. That is the longest stretch a scientist ever documented a teenager staying awake. We will meet him later. It is the reason this story is both amazing and a warning.
Phase one: the first twenty-four hours.
You skip your bedtime, and for a while everything feels fine. You grab a coffee, scroll your phone, and the first night even feels a little exciting. Nothing seems wrong, and that is exactly the trap.
Around sixteen hours after waking up, your attention starts to slip. Studies show that after this point the number of tiny lapses in focus rises sharply. It is the first quiet warning, and most people simply ignore it.
After about seventeen to nineteen hours awake, your reaction time and coordination are about as bad as if you had a blood alcohol level of 0.05 percent.
That level is already the legal driving limit in many countries. You feel completely sober, but your brain is working like it had a few drinks. It is why tired driving is so dangerous: the person behind the wheel is the last one to realise it.
By the time a full day has passed, your hand and eye coordination can be as poor as at 0.1 percent, which is over the legal limit almost everywhere.
Your memory gets leaky too. Information from the day does not get filed properly, so you forget names, appointments and the sentence you just started.
Your thinking takes shortcuts as well. You read the same sentence three times and still cannot say what it meant, and a simple email suddenly feels like a puzzle.
Phase two: thirty-six hours.
Now your body starts to protest. Stress hormones rise, your heart rate and blood pressure creep up, and your body is stuck in a low-level alarm that never switches off. It is a survival response, and it was never designed to run for days on end.
Your hunger hormones flip as well. The hormone that says you are full drops, and the one that says you are hungry rises, so you crave sugar and fatty food.
Your body temperature also dips a little, so you feel cold even in a warm room, because the system that regulates heat is running on empty as well.
Your mood becomes a roller coaster. Small problems feel enormous, jokes stop being funny, and you snap at people you love without really knowing why.
Your immune system weakens too. In experiments, people with too little sleep produced fewer protective antibodies after a vaccine than people who slept well. So the person who brags about never sleeping is often the one who catches every cold going around.
Then come the microsleeps. Your brain falls asleep for a few seconds while your eyes are still open, and you do not even notice it happened.
Imagine this behind the wheel. Four seconds of microsleep at highway speed means you travel the length of a football pitch with nobody driving the car.
Phase three: seventy-two hours.
After three days awake the real strangeness begins. Your brain starts mixing dreams into the waking world, and shadows seem to move at the edge of your vision. Reality feels thin, like a movie that you are slowly losing the plot of.
In 1959 a radio host named Peter Tripp stayed awake for about two hundred hours on a stage in Times Square to raise money for charity.
Doctors watched him closely. By the end he saw spiders in his shoes, believed his doctor was an impostor, and was convinced people were plotting against him. He later said that the experience changed his personality for years.
Thinking itself breaks down. Planning, decision making and impulse control, the jobs of your front brain, are the first things to fail when you stay awake this long.
Emotions run wild too. In a brain scan study at Berkeley, the fear center of sleepless people reacted about sixty percent more strongly to upsetting pictures than in rested people.
Yet some parts of the brain keep going. Tests show that in the sleepless mind, areas for basic attention stay active while the logic centers go dark. That mix is why a person can look awake and still be completely lost.
Almost nobody makes it here by choice. For most of us, the body forces sleep long before this point, whether we like it or not.
Phase four: the world record.
In December 1963 a seventeen year old high school student named Randy Gardner decided to stay awake for his science fair project.
He had two friends to keep him moving, and later a Stanford sleep researcher, William Dement, came to watch and help keep him awake with basketball and pinball.
By the third day he felt sick and had trouble remembering things. His speech got slow, and simple tasks like counting backwards became a struggle. The friends around him had to keep him moving, because sitting down for just a moment would make him drift off.
He finished after eleven days and twenty-five minutes, about two hundred and sixty-four hours, and a navy doctor monitored his health the whole time.
Then he finally slept for about fourteen hours, and woke up groggy but surprisingly normal. His body had survived, and that surprised a lot of people.
The story says that on the last night Randy still beat Dement at pinball, even though he could barely hold a conversation. Simple, trained skills can survive when complex thinking cannot.
Guinness stopped accepting sleep deprivation records in 1997, because the danger was simply too high. The record stands, but nobody is allowed to officially beat it.
Gardner later said that he developed insomnia in adulthood, but nobody can say if the experiment caused it. The long term effects remain unknown.
Phase five: the point of no return.
So can you actually die from it? In humans, nobody has ever been proven to die from simply staying awake, but animal studies show how dangerous it can become.
In famous experiments in the nineteen eighties, rats were kept awake and died after eleven to thirty-two days. They ate more than usual and still lost weight. Scientists were shocked by how quickly a healthy animal could fall apart.
Their bodies lost the ability to control temperature, they developed sores, and their immune systems collapsed. The animals did not die of a single wound, but of systems failing together.
There is also a rare human illness that shows what total sleeplessness can do. It is called fatal familial insomnia, and it is caused by a gene mutation.
In this disease a faulty protein slowly destroys the part of the brain that controls sleep. Patients sleep less and less, and most die within about eighteen months. There is no cure for it, and doctors can only try to make patients comfortable.
It is extraordinarily rare, found in only a few dozen families worldwide, but it proves a terrifying point: without sleep, the human body cannot survive for long.
So the honest answer is yes, total sleep loss would kill you, but your own brain will almost certainly shut you down long before it gets that far.
Phase six: the everyday danger.
The real risk is not an eleven day record attempt. It is the ordinary, quiet loss of one or two hours every night for years. It is also the habit that almost everybody around you has.
Large reviews of studies found that people who regularly sleep too little have a higher risk of heart disease, high blood pressure and diabetes. These are slow, silent risks, which is exactly why people shrug them off.
One big review found that short sleepers had roughly a twelve percent higher risk of dying early than people who slept seven to nine hours.
Then there is the road. Drowsy driving causes crashes every single day, and safety groups believe official numbers are far too low, because tired drivers rarely admit it.
Every hour of sleep you lose also needs more than an hour to recover, and sleeping in at the weekend does not fully repay a week of debt.
And you can be the last to notice. Studies show that people who are sleep deprived rate themselves as only slightly impaired, while their tests show something much worse.
Sleep also decides how well you learn. Students who sleep well after studying remember more, because sleep is when the brain stores the day's lessons for the long term.
Phase seven: how to protect yourself.
Most adults need around seven to nine hours of sleep. This is not a luxury or a lazy habit, it is the daily maintenance your brain and body run on. Children and teenagers need even more, because their brains are still growing.
Try to wake up at the same time every day, even on weekends. A steady rhythm is the strongest signal you can give your inner clock.
Get bright daylight soon after you wake up, and dim your lights in the evening. Light is the main switch that tells your brain when night begins.
Keep your bedroom cool, dark and quiet, and leave your phone outside the bed. A cool room around eighteen degrees Celsius helps most people fall asleep faster.
Treat the last hour before bed like a runway: dim lights, a warm shower, no stressful chats, and your brain gets a clear signal that the landing is coming.
Caffeine stays in your body for hours. One study found that coffee even six hours before bed cost people about an hour of sleep, so stop earlier in the day. Alcohol is no better, because it makes you fall asleep fast and then wrecks the second half of the night.
If you need a nap, keep it short, around twenty minutes, and not too late in the afternoon, so that your night sleep is not stolen.
If you are always exhausted, snore loudly, or wake up gasping, do not push through it. Those can be signs of a sleep disorder, and doctors can really help. A short visit to a doctor can change your whole day, and honestly your whole year.
So, can not sleeping kill you? In the long run, yes, in many quiet ways, and in the short run it can make you dangerous on the road. Both answers are good reasons to take your pillow seriously.
You now know what happens hour by hour, from the first foggy afternoon to the edge of what a human body can survive. It is a fragile and amazing machine, and it asks for only a few hours of rest each day.
Tonight, think of sleep as the most important thing you do, not the thing you do when everything else is finished.
