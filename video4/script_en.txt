# Video 4: Your life as every rank in investment banking, from summer analyst to managing director
# One line = one scene = one image (about 30 words per scene, 12+ minutes). Numbers are rough US examples from public salary surveys and news reports (2025/2026); they differ by bank, city and year. No financial advice.
It is 6:45 on a Monday morning, and you are standing in a glass tower in Manhattan, holding a badge with your name spelled correctly for the first time in your life.
By the end of this video you will have lived through seven ranks of an investment bank, from your very first summer to a corner office that looks out over the entire city.
This is one of the most famous careers in the world, and one of the most brutal. It pays more than almost any other job at your age, and it takes something in return.
All numbers here are rough examples for the United States, based on public salary surveys and news reports. They change by bank, by city and by year. This is not financial advice.
But first, what does an investment bank actually do? It does not keep your savings like your local bank. It advises companies when they want to buy another company, sell themselves, or raise huge amounts of money.
For every deal the bank charges a fee, often millions of dollars. That fee is what pays every salary in this video, and it is why the bank wants its people working day and night. Think of it as a toll booth on the biggest transactions in the economy.
So take a deep breath, check your calendar, which is already full, and let us start at the very bottom.
Phase one: the summer analyst.
You are twenty years old, you survived a brutal interview process, and you have ten weeks to prove that the bank should hire you full time. Most interns in a good program get an offer, but not all of them.
Your job is to build spreadsheets, format slides late into the evening, and find the one tiny mistake that nobody else would ever notice. In this world, the details are the whole job.
The pay is surprisingly good for a student, often several thousand dollars a month before tax. You earn it, because some nights you do not get home until three in the morning.
Almost every evening a senior person sends you a message that starts with the word quick. You slowly learn that quick can mean four hours. Nobody warns you about that on the first day, and everybody learns it by the end of the first week.
But you also see something exciting: real deals, real companies, and people who talk about billion dollar decisions the way other people talk about lunch.
At the end of the summer you get an offer, a start date and a signing bonus. You have just stepped into the machine, and the machine is happy to have you.
Phase two: the first year analyst.
Your base salary is around one hundred and ten thousand dollars, and your year end bonus can add roughly seventy to one hundred and ten thousand more, according to recent industry surveys.
That sounds like a fortune, until you divide it by your hours. A famous internal survey at Goldman Sachs in 2021 reported first year analysts working more than ninety five hours a week on average.
The same group of about a dozen analysts said they slept around five hours a night, and asked for a cap of eighty hours a week. The bank said it was listening.
Your weekends are not really weekends. Some banks now protect the time from Friday night to Sunday morning, but when a deal is live, the deal always wins.
Your life shrinks to three places: your desk, your apartment, and the back seat of a car that the bank pays for after nine in the evening.
You eat dinner at your keyboard, you cancel plans so often that friends stop inviting you, and you start to measure your life in turns of a presentation.
Then one night a client calls, the numbers change, and you rebuild an entire model in two hours. Your boss says only: good. For you, it feels like winning a medal.
Slowly you notice that you have become fast, calm under pressure, and very hard to surprise. That skill is what the bank is really paying you for.
Phase three: the second and third year analyst.
Your base rises to around one hundred and twenty five thousand dollars in year two, and total pay can reach roughly two hundred and ten to two hundred and sixty five thousand.
In a bank with a third year program, you can earn around three hundred thousand dollars in total, at an age when most friends are still paying off student loans.
Now you are the person who trains the new analysts. You correct their models, you tell them which senior bankers are kind, and which ones to avoid on a Friday afternoon.
You also start to get calls from recruiters. Private equity funds and hedge funds love analysts who survived two years of this, and some offer jobs that start months in advance.
This is the moment of the big choice. Stay for the next rank, or leave for a fund, a startup, or a normal life with normal hours.
Many people leave here, and that is not failure. A large share of bankers never stay long enough to be promoted into the revenue generating ranks.
You stay. You take the promotion and learn that you now have a new title, a new salary, and a brand new type of problem.
Phase four: the associate.
Your base salary jumps to around one hundred and seventy five thousand dollars, and a first year associate can earn roughly two hundred and seventy five to three hundred and seventy five thousand in total.
For the first time, you do not only build the model. You manage the analysts who build it, and you are the one who answers when the senior banker asks why the numbers changed.
Your hours drop a little, but your responsibility doubles. When an analyst makes a mistake at two in the morning, it is your name that appears on the email.
A typical week is a strange mix. You plan the work of a whole team, you check every slide before it goes to a client, and you answer a dozen messages while you are still in the elevator.
Part of your bonus now comes as deferred pay, which means it arrives later and only if you are still at the bank. It is a golden leash, and it gets heavier at every rank.
Many associates go back to school for an MBA in this phase, and then return to a bank, or move to private equity, where the pay and the hours look completely different.
You talk to clients now. You learn that nobody wants a lecture about financial theory. They want one calm person who tells them what to do next.
By your third year as an associate you earn around four hundred thousand dollars or more, and you notice that you finally sleep six hours on most nights.
One day your boss pulls you into a meeting room and says the three words that matter most in this business: we are promoting you.
Phase five: the vice president.
Your base is around two hundred and fifty to three hundred thousand dollars, and total pay is often quoted between five hundred and seven hundred thousand dollars a year.
Typical hours fall to around fifty five to sixty five a week. It is still a lot, but you finally see your friends again, and you remember what a Saturday feels like.
Your job changes completely. You are no longer judged on how well you build, but on how well you run a deal team and keep every client happy.
You also meet the real pressure of the business: revenue. Banks pay for people who can bring in deals, and every year somebody counts how much money you helped to earn.
Many people who reach this rank stay here for years, because the next steps are much harder. Not everyone who becomes a vice president ever becomes a director.
The people above you are watching how you handle a crisis. A client who is angry at ten at night tells them more about you than a perfect presentation ever could.
You begin to sell. You attend dinners, you shake hands at conferences, and you learn that the best deals often start with a simple coffee and a phone call.
A senior banker retires, a client follows him to another bank, and the whole team trembles. You see how fragile this business is behind the glass.
But you keep delivering. Deal after deal, year after year, you become the person that clients ask for by name, and the bank starts to notice.
Phase six: the director.
Directors, sometimes called senior vice presidents, earn roughly three hundred to three hundred and fifty thousand dollars in base salary, and around six hundred to eight hundred and fifty thousand in total pay.
The bonus is now more than half of your income, which means your year depends on how the market behaves. A strong year feels like a miracle, a weak year feels like a verdict.
You are not building anything anymore. You are the person who finds the clients, who knows who is thinking about buying or selling a company, and who gets the call first.
Your phone never stops, but the topics change. Instead of formulas you talk about trust, timing and relationships, and you learn that the golden rule of the business is simple: people hire people they like.
Younger bankers watch you the way you once watched your own bosses. You realize that some of them want your job, and that is exactly how the system is designed.
Directors who bring in deals consistently are the ones who get the last promotion. Everything you did for fifteen years is now being summed up in a single decision.
Phase seven: the managing director.
Managing directors earn a base salary of roughly four to six hundred thousand dollars, and total pay is often quoted from eight hundred thousand to well over one million six hundred thousand.
In a very good year the bonus alone can be above a million dollars. In a bad year it can be much smaller, and nobody is guaranteed anything.
Your day looks very different now. You fly to meetings, you meet chief executives, and you spend more time having lunch with clients than looking at a spreadsheet. Even your free time starts to look like a calendar of obligations.
You also carry something heavy: your whole team depends on the deals you win, and younger bankers are watching to see whether you deserve the title. It is no longer only your own career on the line, but theirs too.
You made it. The corner office, the view, the respect. But look closely at the people around you and you notice that very few of the people who started with you are still here.
On quiet evenings you sometimes think about the price. The birthdays you missed, the holidays you worked through, and the friends you only see twice a year. Nobody sends you a bill for these things, but you pay them anyway.
You think back to that Monday morning at 6:45, with your badge and your nervous smile, and you realize how long the road really was.
So was it worth it? For some people, absolutely. For others, the best decision they ever made was to walk out in year two and build a different life. There is no universal answer, only the one that fits the life you want.
Here is the honest summary. Investment banking can give you money, skill and doors that open anywhere, but it asks for your evenings, your weekends and often your twenties.
If you love deals, pressure and numbers, it can be an incredible ladder. If you only want the money, there are easier ways to build wealth, and your health is not for sale. Be honest with yourself about which of the two describes you.
