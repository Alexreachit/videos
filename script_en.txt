# Video 1 (English): Life as an airline pilot, from training to retirement
# One line = one scene = one image. Lines starting with # are ignored.
Your alarm goes off at 3:40 in the morning, and outside it is still pitch dark. You rub your eyes and think one thing: coffee, now.
Twelve hours later you are sitting eleven kilometers up, the sun is rising, and the clouds below you look like a giant carpet.
Sounds like the best job in the world, right? Many people dream of it, but very few know what the daily reality looks like.
It is one of the best. But the road there is longer, more expensive and tougher than most people think.
In this video we follow you through the whole life of an airline pilot, from the very first application to the very last flight.
Phase one: the dream and the first reality check.
Almost every pilot tells the same story: as a kid he watched a plane fly over, and never stopped looking up.
But before you even see the inside of a plane, you have to visit the aviation doctor.
The class one medical certificate is the first big hurdle. Your body decides whether you even get to start.
Then comes the aptitude test: concentration, mental math, multitasking and teamwork, all at once and under pressure.
Many fail right here. Not because they are bad, but because they walk into the room completely unprepared.
Whoever passes gets a place at a flight school. And right after that, a bill.
Phase two: the training.
An integrated training program at a large school costs roughly 100,000 to 180,000 euros, and living costs are not even included.
The route through separate modules is often cheaper, but it still lands in the high five figures.
In return you get about two years of full time training, where you do practically nothing else but study and fly.
First comes the theory: meteorology, navigation, air law, aircraft technology, fourteen exam subjects in total.
You learn why an airplane flies at all. And how many things can theoretically go wrong while it does.
Then, finally, the practice: small propeller planes, tight seats, and an instructor who notices every single mistake.
And then comes the day when your instructor suddenly gets out of the plane.
Your first solo flight. One lap around the airfield, no spectators, no safety net, full responsibility.
You land. Your knees are shaking. And you grin as if you had won the lottery.
After that come night flying, instrument flying and the simulator, where you learn to work as a team in the cockpit.
If everything goes well, after about two years you hold your license in your hands. That feeling is priceless.
Phase three: you have the license. But no job yet.
On paper you are a pilot now. In practice you have just a few hundred flight hours.
And the market swings: in crises, like the pandemic, airlines stop hiring, and applicants sometimes wait a very long time.
Many airlines also only want you once you hold a type rating for their aircraft model.
Depending on the type, that costs around 15,000 to 40,000 euros.
Some airlines pay for it. Others send you the bill or tie you down with a contract.
So read your contract carefully. Really carefully.
After months of applications, the offer finally arrives. You are a first officer.
Phase four: the right seat.
Your first flight with real passengers feels like an exam that never ends. You greet everyone over the microphone and hope your voice does not shake.
An experienced captain sits next to you and watches every move, even if he looks completely relaxed. You take turns: sometimes you fly, sometimes you monitor, but the captain always stays in charge.
Before every flight: weather, fuel, loading and the briefing with the crew.
Then engines on, checklists, radio, and you hardly have time to be nervous.
Below roughly ten thousand feet, the cockpit stays focused on flying. No jokes, no small talk.
The autopilot does a lot. But takeoff, landing and every important decision stay with the two of you.
And your roster? It runs your life.
Early shift, late shift, night flight. Rome today, Hamburg tomorrow, and the day after a hotel room you only see to sleep in. In the evening you eat with the crew and count the days until your next free weekend.
There are clear rules for this: at most 900 flight hours per calendar year and fixed minimum rest times between duties.
Safety comes first. Tired pilots are dangerous pilots, so rest is not an option, it is a duty. Rules like these are part of why flying is one of the safest ways to travel.
A small myth on the side: many airlines recommend that the captain and the first officer do not eat the same meal.
The reason: if one dish does not agree with someone, the whole cockpit does not go down.
Twice a year the simulator is waiting: engine failure, storm, system error. Everything is practiced until it works in your sleep.
At every check an examiner sits behind you and watches your hands. Your job always depends on your performance.
On top of that comes the medical exam, at least once a year, and after sixty even every six months.
Phase five: moments when it gets serious.
A thunderstorm on your route. You fly around it, which costs time and fuel, but never safety.
A go around shortly before landing because a vehicle is still on the runway. Power up, climb, try again.
The passengers hardly notice, and that is exactly the goal. Behind the door a team is working that has talked through every step beforehand.
You learn to stay calm. And when in doubt, to go around once too often rather than once too rarely.
Phase six: the fourth stripe.
Depending on the airline it takes many years until you sit on the left, and you usually need several thousand flight hours.
Then comes the upgrade: a new course, new exams, new responsibility.
As a captain, you decide in the end. Whether you take off, whether you turn back, whether you land.
Your first flight as commander feels like your first solo flight, only with over a hundred people behind you.
And over time you become exactly the captain you once admired. You explain to the new first officer how to stay calm, just as someone once explained it to you.
The price: you miss birthdays, weddings and sometimes even Christmas.
Long haul means jet lag, time zones and nights when your body no longer knows what day it is.
But there is also the side that money cannot buy: sunrises over the Atlantic and cities at night seen from above.
And days off in the middle of the week, when everyone else sits in the office and looks enviously at their phones.
The salary? Good, but strongly dependent on the airline. At big companies it gets really solid as a captain, at others it varies a lot.
Phase seven: the end of the career.
In airline operations it ends at sixty five. Until then you have to keep passing every check, and after sixty the medical exams come even more often.
At some big airlines captains leave earlier, with a transition pension from the company.
At smaller companies you usually have to save on your own, so that the gap until retirement does not hurt.
Your last flight is something special: colleagues, family and often a water cannon salute from fire trucks.
You taxi to the gate, the engines shut down. Thirty years, thousands of flights, a whole life in the cockpit.
And somewhere in the terminal stands a teenager seeing a plane for the very first time. And dreaming.
Is it worth it? If you love flying and do not shy away from responsibility: yes.
But go in with open eyes. Plan for money, time and setbacks from the very start.
